\begin{abstract}
I compare a single decision tree, bagging and SAMME multiclass boosting on raw-pixel
Olivetti faces (40 identities), with bagging and boosting written from scratch.
Models are trained on clean images only and tested on clean faces and on faces with a
centred $16\times16$ block set to zero. Bias$^2$ and variance are then measured over 30
within-identity resamples of the training set.
Bagging reaches $91.25\%$ clean test accuracy against $61.25\%$ for the tuned tree and removes
$97\%$ of its variance, while its measured bias$^2$ rises slightly.
Boosting has the lowest variance but the lowest accuracy.
\end{abstract}

% =====================================================================
\section{Setup}
Olivetti has 400 images of $64\times64$ pixels, 10 for each of 40 identities.
The seed is every digit of the roll number, $260009$, given to \texttt{numpy.random.default\_rng}.
Each identity's 10 images are split at random into 6 train, 2 validation and 2 test images
($240/80/80$); the split is saved and reused everywhere.
Features are raw flattened pixels, with no PCA or other reduction.
The masked test set sets rows and columns 24--39 of each $64\times64$ test image to zero
before flattening. All models train on clean images only.
\texttt{DecisionTreeClassifier} is the only scikit-learn model used; bagging and SAMME are my own code.
Ties in validation accuracy go to the earlier (shallower, smaller-leaf, fewer-learner) setting, and
standard deviations use \texttt{np.std} with \texttt{ddof=0}.

\subsection{Diagnostics and choice of $B$ (Q1c)}
Validation accuracy is highest at $B=200$ ($0.9250$), so $B=200$ is the final size. $B=50$ and $B=100$ tie at $0.8875$,
so the lead of $B=200$ is three validation images out of 80.

The three diagnostics do not tell the same story (Figure~\ref{fig:q1c}).
OOB accuracy ($0.34\to0.77$) and vote margin ($0.25\to0.30$) keep rising with $B$, while
pairwise disagreement stays near $0.78$ for all $B\ge5$. Disagreement describes how different individual trees are,
which does not change as trees are added, so it cannot say when to stop.
OOB accuracy trails validation accuracy at small $B$ because a training image is out-of-bag for only about $37\%$ of the trees,
so its OOB vote comes from roughly $0.37B$ trees and is noisier.

\section{Q2: SAMME boosting}

\subsection{Toy round (Q2a)}
SAMME uses $\alpha_t=\log\frac{1-\epsilon_t}{\epsilon_t}+\log(K-1)$, $S_k(x)=\sum_t\alpha_t\mathbb{1}[h_t(x)=k]$ and
confidence $q_k(x)=S_k(x)/\sum_t\alpha_t$.
A weak learner with $\epsilon_t\ge1-1/K$ is no better than random multiclass guessing; the code logs it, discards it and stops
(keeping it only if it is the first learner), and a learner with $\epsilon_t=0$ ends boosting after it is added.
The toy example and the face model call the same update function. The toy set has three classes, ten samples and a depth-1 stump:

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{ll}
initial weights $w_0$ & $0.1$ for all 10 samples\\
stump predictions & $[0,0,0,0,0,0,2,2,2,2]$\\
misclassified & samples 4--6 (class 1, counting from 1)\\
weighted error $\epsilon_1$ & $0.30$\\
$\alpha_1$ & $\log(0.7/0.3)+\log2=1.5404$\\
updated weights & $0.0476$ (correct), $0.2222$ (wrong)\\
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\paragraph{A heavily weighted face.}
Training image 177 (identity 29) reaches the largest weight of any sample, $0.0837$ at round 91, about $20\times$ the uniform $1/240$.
It is misclassified in 87 of the 100 rounds. In the first 20 rounds it is correct only at rounds 4, 9 and 16, and each time its weight
drops sharply at the next round (for example $0.0061\to0.0008$ after round 4), then climbs again over the following rounds because
later learners get it wrong. A misclassified sample's weight is multiplied by $e^{\alpha_t}$ before renormalising, so a face that shallow learners
keep missing is pushed up and forces the next learner to attend to it again, which is why boosting keeps returning to it.

\section{Q3: Bias and variance}
I made 30 new training sets by drawing six faces with replacement, within each identity, from its six original training faces
(about $4.0$ distinct faces per identity on average, $6(1-(5/6)^6)$). The clean test set is fixed. I refit the selected tree (depth None, leaf 1),
the final bagged model ($B=200$) and the selected boosting model (depth 4, 100 rounds) with no re-tuning.
The class vector $p_r(x_i)$ is the one-hot prediction for the tree, the vote fraction for bagging and the normalised weighted vote for boosting.
\begin{equation}
\text{Bias}^2=\tfrac1N\sum_i\|\bar p(x_i)-e_i\|_2^2,\quad
\text{Var}=\tfrac1{NR}\sum_{i,r}\|p_r(x_i)-\bar p(x_i)\|_2^2 .
\end{equation}

Accuracy under resampling is below that of the frozen models (tree $0.469$ vs $0.6125$, bagging $0.8025$ vs $0.9125$) because each resample has only about four distinct faces per identity instead of six.
Bagging removes $97\%$ of the tree's variance ($0.645\to0.020$), but its bias$^2$ is higher ($0.418\to0.504$) although its accuracy is much higher.
Bias$^2$ here is the squared distance between the mean class vector and the one-hot target. A tree's one-hot outputs can average close to the target,
while bagged vote fractions are spread over several classes (mean winning vote about $0.4$), which costs squared error even when the argmax is correct.
Boosting's variance is low for a similar reason: its normalised votes are nearly flat (mean winning probability about $0.12$), so they barely change between refits, and its bias$^2$ is the largest ($0.89$).

\subsection{Is the deepest tree where bagging helps most? (Q3c)}
In this experiment yes: the accuracy gain from bagging is $+0.089$, $+0.261$ and $+0.322$ at depths 2, 8 and None, but the measurements show why this need not hold in general.
The gain tracks the variance available to remove ($0.068$, $0.277$, $0.645$), not depth itself.
The shallow trees have 3 and 9 leaves and the highest bias$^2$ ($1.82$, $1.40$), so most of their error is systematic and averaging cannot remove it (bagged depth-2 accuracy is only $0.148$).
The deep tree has 46 leaves with about five training images each and $13\%$ single-image leaves, so its prediction depends on which few images were drawn, which is the variance bagging averages out.
Disagreement among bagged trees is lowest at the deepest setting ($0.782$ against $0.955$ at depth 2), so removing variance does not need high disagreement here.
I measured only three depths, so I cannot say where between 8 and None the largest gain occurs.

\paragraph{Sensitivity to the missing region.}
In the refit experiment bagging loses the most in absolute terms ($0.283$, against $0.155$ for the tree and $0.026$ for boosting), but boosting's small drop is a floor effect since it starts at $0.30$.
In the frozen comparison (Table~\ref{tab:q2d}) the drops are $0.3375$ for bagging and $0.2125$ for both the tree and boosting. Bagging still has the best masked accuracy in both experiments.

\paragraph{Lowest variance versus best accuracy.}
Boosting has the lowest variance, the lowest accuracy and the highest bias$^2$; bagging has slightly higher variance and far better accuracy.
A model that is consistently wrong is stable, so variance alone does not rank models.
